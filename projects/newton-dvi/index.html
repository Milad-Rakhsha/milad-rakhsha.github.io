Newton DVI: maximal-coordinate dynamics for robot learning

Multibody dynamics · GPU simulation

My work on Newton DVI examines how to organize constrained multibody dynamics for GPU execution. The solver uses maximal coordinates, with bilateral joints and unilateral contact represented in constraint space. Here I outline how that representation differs from the reduced-coordinate approach used by MuJoCo, and why the distinction matters for articulated robots and closed-loop mechanisms.

DVI project page ↗

Maximal and reduced coordinates

In a maximal-coordinate formulation, each dynamic rigid body has an independent pose and a six-component spatial velocity. Joints impose constraints between bodies rather than eliminating their relative degrees of freedom through the state representation. The body-space mass matrix is block diagonal, while coupling between bodies enters through the constraint Jacobian.

MuJoCo uses generalized joint coordinates for a kinematic tree. Tree joints are built into the parameterization, reducing the velocity-space dimension. Closed-loop connections are represented using additional equality constraints. This changes where the computational work appears: tree kinematics and the joint-space inertia replace the explicit treatment of all articulation joints in a maximal-coordinate constraint system. From body dynamics to constraint space

At a fixed configuration, the velocity update can be written schematically in impulse form as

vn+1 = vfree + M−1JTλ.

Here, M is the body-space mass matrix, J is the constraint Jacobian, and λ contains constraint impulses. Substituting this update into the constraint equations introduces the Delassus operator:

D = JM−1JT.

The report’s semi-implicit time step and convex relaxation of Coulomb friction lead to a cone-constrained quadratic program. Bilateral impulses are unrestricted, while contact impulses are constrained by the admissible friction cones. Compliance and numerical regularization modify the constraint-space operator.

A decoupled bilateral–unilateral solve

I learned a great deal about practical solver design from Maciej Mizerski during my time at Roblox. His article on Roblox’s physics solver and GDC talk, Improving an Iterative Physics Solver Using a Direct Method, explain how a direct solve for equality constraints can be combined with iterative constraint solving. Those lessons informed how I approached this work.

The contribution I focus on here is a fully GPU-resident implementation of the DVI solve: both the sparse bilateral factorization and the matrix-free unilateral iterations run on the GPU. I designed this pipeline for batched robot simulation, keeping the solve on the device and supporting repeated execution through CUDA graphs.

The implementation described in the report separates the persistent bilateral joint graph from the changing unilateral constraints. The bilateral subsystem uses a GPU block-sparse LDLT factorization with fill-reducing reorderings. Joint limits and frictional contacts use matrix-free projected iterations: projected Jacobi, accelerated projected gradient (APGD), or preconditioned spectral projected gradient with fallback (P-SPG-FB).

Matrix-free application evaluates the Delassus action through Jacobian products and inverse body inertia rather than assembling the full operator. Optional alternating block sweeps retain more of the bilateral–unilateral coupling. The DVI method is also available as an optional backend in Newton’s Kamino framework.

Implications for robot simulation

For a tree-structured robot, reduced coordinates avoid explicitly solving for the constraints associated with every articulation joint. For a maximal-coordinate system, closed loops fit directly into the bilateral graph, but factorization cost depends on its connectivity and elimination fill. Coordinate count alone therefore does not determine throughput; topology, contact workload, solver tolerance, and batching also matter.

Coordinate representation is also separate from the choice of contact model. Comparing DVI and MuJoCo requires accounting for both, along with time integration and solver settings. A maximal-coordinate formulation does not by itself imply a particular friction law or convergence behavior.

Policy transfer and experimental results

The report evaluates numerical accuracy, contact-solver convergence, batched simulation, and policy learning. It also reports transfer of DVI-trained policies to MuJoCo-C and a physical Unitree Go2 on rough terrain. The project page collects the detailed results and demonstrations.
